# Professional communication

A professional message is judged twice: once on whether the reader can act on it in
under a minute, and again, months later, when it is forwarded, quoted or read in a
dispute. Good messages survive both. Most bad ones fail by burying the ask, by
hedging until the point disappears, or by letting mood leak into a record. This skill
optimises for a reader who is busy, who may not share your context, and who will
remember how the message made them feel longer than what it said.

## Before you start

1. Read the brand profile for voice, point of view, spelling, contractions, and the
   tone row for the relevant situation (support, billing, sales). If the message is
   personal rather than on behalf of a brand, the user's own preferences override.
2. Establish, in one line each:
   - **Reader**: who, their seniority relative to the sender, what they know already.
   - **Outcome**: what should be true after they read it (a decision, a date, a file,
     nothing but goodwill).
   - **Relationship temperature**: new, warm, strained, formal by contract.
   - **Record risk**: could this be forwarded, quoted, or read by a lawyer?
3. If replying, read the whole thread. Answer what was actually asked before
   adding anything.
4. Identify the message type and open the matching section of
   `references/message-types.md`.

## Principles

### 1. The ask goes first
State the decision, request or news in the first two lines. Context follows for
those who need it. A reader on a phone sees the first 80 characters and the first
sentence; if the ask is in paragraph three, it did not arrive.

### 2. Make the next action unmissable
End with exactly what you need, from whom, by when. One ask per message where
possible. If there are several, number them. "Let me know your thoughts" is not an
ask.

### 3. Write for the forward
Assume the message will be read by someone who was not on the thread. No sarcasm,
no in-jokes about third parties, nothing about a person you would not say to them.
Facts, dates, and what was agreed. If you are angry, write the message, wait, and
cut every sentence that exists to make you feel better.

### 4. Short, but not curt
Length signals effort for difficult news and disrespect for routine requests.
Routine: three to five lines. Difficult: as long as it takes to be clear and humane,
never longer. Warmth comes from acknowledging the reader's situation in one
specific sentence, not from adjectives or exclamation marks.

### 5. Match their register, lead by a notch
Mirror the reader's formality, then be one notch warmer or clearer than they were.
Never more formal than the relationship, never more casual than the record allows.
Use their name once. Use the sign-off the profile or the relationship expects.

### 6. Own it plainly
Bad news, delays and mistakes are stated in the first line in the active voice, with
what happens next. No "mistakes were made", no "unfortunately due to circumstances".
Apologise once, specifically, then move to the fix. Over-apologising transfers the
discomfort to the reader.

## Process

1. Write the reader/outcome/temperature/record line.
2. Write the ask or news as one sentence. That is line one or two.
3. Add only the context the reader needs to say yes or act. Cut the rest.
4. Write the closing action: who, what, by when.
5. Subject line last: the ask or the news, under 50 characters, searchable later
   ("Contract renewal: sign by Fri 3 Oct", not "Quick question").
6. Read as the recipient, once as their best self and once as their worst. Fix
   anything the worst reading could use.
7. Run the checklist.

## Checklist before delivering

- [ ] Ask or news appears in the first two lines
- [ ] Closing states who does what by when
- [ ] Subject line names the topic and any deadline, under 50 characters
- [ ] Nothing about a third party you would not say to them
- [ ] No sarcasm, no sentence that exists to vent
- [ ] Apology, if any, appears once and is followed by the fix
- [ ] No "just", "quick", "sorry to bother", "hope this finds you well"
- [ ] Register matches the reader; name used once; sign-off fits the relationship
- [ ] Routine message under 120 words
- [ ] Every date has a day of the week and a timezone if cross-border

## Anti-patterns

- **The preamble.** Two paragraphs of pleasantries before the point.
- **The hedge sandwich.** "I was just wondering if maybe it might be possible to..."
- **The thoughts request.** "Let me know your thoughts" as the only ask.
- **The CC weapon.** Adding a manager to a thread to apply pressure without saying so.
- **The passive apology.** "Apologies for any inconvenience this may have caused."
- **Reply-all narration.** Answering a question to twelve people who did not ask it.

## Example

**Before**

> Subject: Quick question
>
> Hi Sam, hope you're doing well and that the week isn't too crazy! I was just
> wondering whether you might have had a chance to look at the proposal we sent
> over a couple of weeks ago? No rush at all, I know things are busy, but it would
> be great to get your thoughts whenever you get a moment. Thanks so much!!

**After**

> Subject: Proposal decision needed by Fri 3 Oct
>
> Hi Sam,
>
> Could you confirm by Friday 3 October whether you'd like to go ahead with the
> proposal from 15 September? If yes, I'll send the contract that day so we can
> start on the 13th as planned. If you need changes, send them and I'll turn a
> revision around within two days.
>
> Thanks,
> Alex

What changed: the ask moved to line one with a date, the hedging went, the subject
became searchable, and the reader knows exactly what happens after each answer.
