# Marketing copy

Marketing copy has one reader at a time and one job: move that reader one step,
whether that is scrolling, clicking, signing up or remembering the name. Most weak
copy fails by talking about the company instead of the reader, by claiming instead
of showing, or by trying to make three points where one would land. This skill
forces the choice of one reader, one promise and one next step, and keeps every
claim honest enough to survive a sceptic and a lawyer.

## Before you start

1. Read the brand profile. You need: voice adjectives and their "but not" pairs,
   tone for the sales situation, allowed and banned claims, preferred and banned
   words, spelling, and the "us at our best" examples. Read those examples before
   writing a word.
2. Establish four things and write them at the top of your draft:
   - **Reader**: one person, their role, what they are doing when they meet this.
   - **Stage**: unaware / problem-aware / solution-aware / comparing / ready to buy.
   - **Promise**: the one outcome they get, in their words.
   - **Next step**: the single action the piece asks for.
3. Identify the format and open the matching section of `references/formats.md`.
4. If the profile is missing, say so once, state assumptions for voice and claims,
   and mark every factual claim `[TODO: source]`.

## Principles

### 1. Reader first, product second
Open with the reader's situation or outcome, not the company or the feature. "You"
before "we". A feature is only worth mentioning as the proof of a benefit the reader
already wants.

### 2. One promise per piece
A page that promises speed, security, savings and simplicity is remembered for none
of them. Choose the promise the reader cares most about at their stage, put it in the
headline, and let everything below support it. Other benefits are supporting cast.

### 3. Specific beats superlative
"Fastest" is a claim; "renders a 40-page report in 1.2 seconds" is a fact and a
picture. Numbers, names, before-and-after, a quoted customer sentence. Every
superlative should be replaced by the evidence that would have justified it, or cut.

### 4. Earn every claim
Only make claims listed as allowed in the brand profile or backed by a source you
can cite. Comparative and absolute claims ("best", "only", "guaranteed") need legal
cover. When in doubt, describe what it does rather than how it ranks.

### 5. Match the voice, then vary the tempo
The brand adjectives set the register. Within it, alternate long and short
sentences, put the strongest word at the end of the line, and cut every word that
does not change meaning or rhythm. Read it aloud. If you would not say it to the
reader across a table, rewrite it.

### 6. One clear next step
Each piece has one primary call to action, phrased as what the reader gets, not what
they do ("Start your free workspace", not "Submit"). Secondary actions are visibly
secondary. Never end a piece without the step.

## Process

1. Write the reader/stage/promise/next-step block.
2. Draft ten headlines. Pick the one that states the promise most concretely; keep
   two alternates for testing.
3. Outline the argument as a sequence of objections the reader will have, in the
   order they will have them. Each section answers one.
4. Draft fast, then cut by a third.
5. Replace every adjective with evidence or delete it. Check every claim against the
   profile's allowed list.
6. Read aloud. Run the checklist. Have `brand-reviewer` check voice, and
   `copy-editor` check mechanics, if the piece is going public.

## Checklist before delivering

- [ ] Headline states the promise in the reader's words, under 10 words
- [ ] First sentence is about the reader, not the company
- [ ] One primary CTA, phrased as an outcome, present at the end
- [ ] No superlative without a cited source
- [ ] No claim outside the brand profile's allowed list
- [ ] No banned words; preferred terms and casing used
- [ ] No sentence over 25 words; average under 15
- [ ] Every feature mentioned is tied to a stated benefit
- [ ] Reads aloud without stumbling
- [ ] Alternates provided for headline and CTA

## Anti-patterns

- **The mirror.** "We are passionate about...", "Our mission is...". The reader did
  not come to hear about you.
- **The buzzword stack.** "Seamless, powerful, AI-driven, next-generation."
  Together they mean nothing.
- **The feature list as page.** Twelve icons with three words each. No argument.
- **The buried CTA.** One button, at the bottom, saying "Learn more".
- **Borrowed voice.** Copy that sounds like the current market leader, or like a
  meme, instead of the brand profile.
- **Fake urgency.** Countdown timers and "only 3 left" for software.

## Example

**Before**

> Acme is the leading AI-powered platform that empowers teams to seamlessly
> collaborate and unlock powerful insights. Our cutting-edge technology is trusted
> by thousands. Learn more.

**After**

> Ship the weekly report without the weekly scramble.
>
> Acme pulls numbers from your tools, drafts the summary, and flags what changed.
> Teams at 1,400 companies send theirs before lunch on Monday.
>
> Start your free workspace →

What changed: one promise in the reader's words, evidence instead of adjectives,
and a CTA phrased as the outcome.
